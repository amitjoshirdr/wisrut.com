1. Objectives and importance of human resource management (Unit I)

Human resource management (HRM) is the work of getting the right people, developing their skills, motivating them and keeping them, so that the organisation reaches its goals and the people also grow. The human resource is the total of the knowledge, skills and effort of the people who work in an organisation.

1.1 Quick notes

  • HRM deals with people, not machines. People have feelings, so they need to be treated fairly.
  • It is a continuous process. It does not end when a person is hired.
  • It is pervasive: every manager in every department does some HR work.
  • It balances the goals of the organisation and the goals of its employees.
  • Earlier it was called personnel management. HRM takes a wider and more future-looking view of the same people.

1.2 Objectives of HRM

TypeMeaningExample
OrganisationalHelp the organisation reach its goals by having the right peopleEnough trained weavers to meet orders
FunctionalKeep the HR department's service at a level the organisation needs, neither too costly nor too weakQuick hiring at a fair cost
SocietalMeet the needs of society ethically and obey the lawFair pay, no child labour
PersonalHelp employees reach their own goalsSkill growth, job security

1.3 Importance of HRM

  • For the organisation: right people in right jobs, higher productivity, lower labour turnover, a good culture and a good name.
  • For employees: fair pay, training, chances to grow, job satisfaction and a safe workplace.
  • For society: jobs, skills, better living standards and fewer disputes.
  • People are the one resource that can learn and improve. Money and machines cannot.
Example. Himalaya Handloom Ltd makes woollen shawls in Dehradun. Orders doubled, but weavers kept leaving because of low pay and no training. The new HR manager fixed pay bands, started a skill programme and began monthly meetings. Within a year, fewer weavers left and output rose. This shows the organisational, personal and functional objectives working together.

2. Challenges of a modern HR manager and broad HR functions (Unit I)

A modern HR manager works in a fast-changing world. New technology, new laws and new expectations of employees make the job harder than before.

2.1 Challenges faced by a modern HR manager

  • Workforce diversity: people of different age, gender, region, language and background work together. Fair treatment of all is a duty.
  • Globalisation: competition and talent come from many countries, so firms must match global standards.
  • Technology and automation: jobs change, and staff must learn new skills. Some jobs disappear.
  • Attrition and talent retention: good employees get better offers and leave.
  • Skill gap: the skills people have are not the skills the firm needs.
  • Changing expectations: employees want work-life balance, flexible hours and meaningful work.
  • Legal changes: labour laws change, so HR rules and records must be updated. India now has four labour codes. [CHECK]
  • Cost control: firms want high quality at low cost, which puts pressure on pay and hiring.
  • Ethics and fairness: avoiding bias, harassment and unfair treatment.
  • Employee engagement and well-being: keeping people interested, healthy and loyal.

2.2 Broad HR functions

HR functions are of two broad kinds: managerial functions (the general management work applied to HR) and operative functions (the specific HR jobs).

Example. At Himalaya Handloom, a shift to power looms meant weavers needed new skills. The HR manager faced three challenges at once: technology change, a skill gap and fear of job loss. The answer used several functions: planning (how many operators?), training, fair pay for the new skills and open communication.

3. HR policies, Indian labour policy and HR planning (Unit II)

An HR policy is a standing guide that tells managers how to decide on people matters, so that similar cases are treated in the same way.

3.1 HR policies: needs and types

Needs of HR policies

  • They give a consistent way of handling similar cases.
  • They make decisions fair and reduce favouritism.
  • They save time, because managers need not ask the top each time.
  • They help new managers and employees know what to expect.
  • They help the organisation follow the law.
  • They support employee morale and trust.
TypeMeaning
OriginatedMade by top management to guide the whole organisation
AppealedMade because a manager asks for a ruling on an unusual case
ImposedForced on the firm by law, government or a trade union
ImpliedNot written, but followed as habit
GeneralApply to the whole organisation
SpecificApply to one matter, such as leave or transfer

A good policy is written, clear, fair, flexible, consistent with the law and known to all. Common policy areas are recruitment, promotion, pay, training, discipline and leave.

3.2 Indian labour policy

Indian labour policy is the set of government principles and laws that protect workers, support fair relations between employers and workers and keep industrial peace.

  • It draws on the Constitution: fair wages, decent working conditions, protection of women and children and a right to work (Directive Principles).
  • It follows tripartism: the government, employers and workers talk together, for example in the Indian Labour Conference.
  • Its aims: industrial peace, worker welfare, social security, fair wages and better productivity.
  • Earlier tools: Five-Year Plans, ILO standards and the Code of Discipline (1958). [CHECK]
  • Recent change: 29 central labour laws have been merged into four labour codes: the Code on Wages, 2019; the Industrial Relations Code, 2020; the Code on Social Security, 2020; and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020. They came into force on 21 November 2025, and the Central Rules were notified on 8 May 2026. State rules are still being notified. [CHECK]

3.3 HR planning

Human resource planning is forecasting how many people, and with what skills, the organisation will need, and planning how to get them at the right time.

Steps in HR planning

  1. Study the plans of the organisation (growth, new products, new plants).
  2. Forecast demand for people: by workload, past trends, ratios or expert opinion.
  3. Audit the present manpower (supply): number, skills, age, retirement and expected turnover.
  4. Find the gap between demand and supply: a shortage or a surplus.
  5. Prepare the action plan: recruitment, training, transfers, promotions, or VRS for surplus.
  6. Carry out the plan.
  7. Monitor and review the results and correct them.

Determinants of HR requirements

  • Business plans, growth and expansion.
  • Level of production and sales.
  • Technology and automation.
  • Retirement, transfers, absenteeism and labour turnover.
  • Skill mix needed.
  • Budget and financial position.
  • Government rules and labour laws.
  • Nature of the industry and market conditions.
Example. Himalaya Handloom plans to open a second unit. It needs 60 more weavers and 4 supervisors. It has 10 weavers who can be moved and 3 supervisors due for promotion. The gap is 50 weavers and 1 supervisor, so the plan is to recruit 50 trainees, promote 3 and hire 1 outside supervisor.

4. Job analysis, recruitment, selection, placement and orientation (Unit II)

This chapter follows the path of one new employee, from the study of the job to the first days at work.

4.1 Job analysis

Job analysis is the study of a job to find out its duties and the qualities a person needs to do it. It gives two outputs:

PointJob descriptionJob specification
TellsWhat the job isWhat the person should be
ContentsTitle, duties, reporting, working conditionsEducation, skills, experience, health, traits
FocusThe jobThe job holder

Methods of collecting data: observation, interview, questionnaire, diary or log kept by the worker. Uses: recruitment, selection, training, pay fixing, appraisal and job design.

4.2 Recruitment

Recruitment is searching for and attracting enough suitable candidates to apply for jobs.

SourceExamples
InternalPromotion, transfer, employee referral, re-hiring past staff
ExternalAdvertisement, campus recruitment, employment exchanges, recruitment agencies, online job portals, walk-ins

Internal sources are cheap and good for morale. External sources bring new ideas and skills.

Recruitment process

  1. Find the vacancy and get approval.
  2. Prepare the job description and job specification.
  3. Decide the sources and the method.
  4. Invite applications.
  5. Screen the applications and prepare a shortlist.

4.3 Selection process

Selection is choosing the best candidate from those who applied.

  1. Preliminary screening of applications and a short interview.
  2. Application form (blank).
  3. Tests: aptitude, skill, knowledge, personality.
  4. Interview.
  5. Checking references and background.
  6. Medical examination.
  7. Final decision by the line manager and HR.
  8. Job offer and appointment letter.
PointRecruitmentSelection
NaturePositive: attracts candidatesNegative: rejects the unfit
AimBuild a pool of applicantsChoose the best from the pool
Ends withReceipt of applicationsAppointment letter

4.4 Placement and orientation

  • Placement is putting the selected person on the job that matches his or her skills and interest. If the fit is wrong, the person can be moved during probation.
  • Orientation (also called induction) is the first introduction of the new employee to the organisation: its history, rules, pay and benefits, safety, the team and the job. It reduces fear, early exits and mistakes.
Example. Himalaya Handloom needs a loom supervisor. The HR team writes a job description (supervise 20 looms, report to the unit head) and a job specification (diploma in textiles, 3 years' experience). It advertises on a job portal and in the local paper, receives 45 applications, shortlists 12, holds a skill test and interview, checks references, makes an offer to one and holds a one-day orientation with a senior supervisor as guide.

5. Training and development, and HRD (Unit III)

Training teaches an employee the skills needed for the present job. Development prepares the person for future and bigger roles. The syllabus text reads "New roles / Holds of Training", which looks like a typing error. This guide covers the changing role and the needs of training. [CHECK]

5.1 Role and needs of training

  • New employees need job skills and knowledge of the firm.
  • Technology and methods change, so skills must be updated.
  • Gaps in performance show up in appraisal.
  • Promotion needs new skills.
  • Training lowers waste, accidents and supervision cost.
  • It builds morale and loyalty. Today training is seen as an investment, not an expense.

5.4 Evaluating the effectiveness of training

Evaluation asks whether the training achieved its aim. A four-level view is often used:

  1. Reaction: did trainees like it? (feedback forms)
  2. Learning: did they learn? (tests before and after)
  3. Behaviour: do they work differently? (observation, supervisor reports)
  4. Results: did output, quality or costs improve? (cost-benefit check)

5.5 HRD: concept and components

Human resource development (HRD) is a planned, continuous effort to build the skills, knowledge, attitude and motivation of employees, and the culture of trust in the organisation.

Components (mechanisms): training, performance appraisal, potential appraisal, career planning, succession planning, organisation development, counselling and feedback, rewards, and quality of work life.

PointTrainingDevelopment
FocusPresent jobFuture growth
TimeShort termLong term
WhoMostly non-managersMostly managers
NatureSkills for a taskWider growth and attitudes

5.6 Training and development facilities in India

  • Industrial Training Institutes (ITIs) and the apprenticeship system under the Apprentices Act, 1961.
  • Skill India programmes and the National Skill Development Corporation, with sector skill councils.
  • Management institutes such as the IIMs and other business schools, and bodies such as the Administrative Staff College of India.
  • Professional bodies: National Institute of Personnel Management and the Indian Society for Training and Development.
  • National Productivity Council and its regional bodies.
  • In-house training centres in large companies and online platforms. [CHECK]
Example. Himalaya Handloom buys power looms. Operators first get on-the-job coaching from a senior (cheap and practical). Supervisors go to a two-day workshop (off-the-job). After three months the firm checks: trainee feedback (reaction), a skill test (learning), fewer yarn breaks seen by supervisors (behaviour) and higher output (results).

6. Performance appraisal and exit of employees (Unit III)

Performance appraisal is the regular evaluation of an employee's work and potential against agreed standards.

6.1 Purposes and steps

  • Used for pay rises, promotion, transfer and training needs.
  • Gives employees feedback on their work.
  • Helps HR planning and the review of selection.
  1. Set performance standards and tell employees.
  2. Measure actual performance.
  3. Compare it with the standards.
  4. Discuss the result with the employee.
  5. Take action: reward, train or correct.

6.2 Methods of appraisal

GroupMethods
TraditionalRanking, paired comparison, grading, graphic rating scale, checklist, forced distribution, critical incident, essay (narrative) report, confidential report
ModernManagement by objectives (MBO), behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS), assessment centre, 360-degree appraisal
  • MBO: the manager and employee agree on goals and later check results.
  • BARS: rating scales with examples of actual behaviour for each rating.
  • Assessment centre: exercises and tests, mostly for choosing managers.

6.3 360-degree appraisal

In a 360-degree appraisal, feedback about a person comes from all around: the boss, peers, subordinates, customers and the person himself (self-appraisal).

  • Advantages: a wider and fairer view, less bias of one boss, shows strengths and gaps, good for development.
  • Limits: takes time and money; people may give dishonest or friendly marks; needs trust and training for raters; may cause conflict.

Common errors in appraisal: halo effect (one good trait colours everything), leniency (everyone rated high), central tendency (everyone rated average), personal bias and recency (only recent events count).

6.4 Exit of employees

Employees leave the organisation in several ways: resignation, retirement, dismissal, layoff, retrenchment, death and voluntary retirement. A good HR team holds an exit interview to learn why people leave.

  • Voluntary retirement scheme (VRS): an offer by the firm that lets employees retire before the normal age, with a payment. It is used to reduce staff without forced job loss.
  • Golden handshake: a very generous lump-sum package offered to encourage senior or well-placed people to leave early.
  • VRS payments up to a limit are tax-exempt under the Income Tax Act for eligible employees. The limit and conditions change. [CHECK]
  • The scheme should be fair, voluntary and open to all in the group. A wrongly designed scheme can make good performers leave.
PointVRSRetrenchment
NatureVoluntaryForced by the employer
Employee consentNeededNot needed
PaymentAttractive packageCompensation as per law
Effect on moraleSmaller shockLarger shock
Example. Himalaya Handloom moves to power looms and has 15 surplus hand-loom workers aged 52 to 58. It offers a VRS with a lump sum and a tailoring kit. Nine accept and six stay and are retrained. There are no forced exits and the union stays calm. Each leaver is asked for an exit interview.

7. Compensation and motivation (Unit IV)

Compensation is everything an employee receives for work: pay, benefits and rewards. A good compensation policy is fair, attracts talent and keeps it.

7.1 Factors affecting compensation policy

  • Ability to pay of the organisation.
  • Demand and supply of that skill in the labour market.
  • Cost of living and inflation.
  • Productivity and performance of the employee.
  • Trade union strength and collective bargaining.
  • Government laws such as minimum wage rules.
  • Job value (job evaluation): skill, effort, responsibility and working conditions.
  • Prevailing pay in similar firms.
  • The firm's size, policy and the employee's experience and qualification.

7.4 Wage policy and wage fixation in India

  • Wage policy of the government aims at a need-based minimum wage, fair pay, a link between pay and productivity and less wide gaps in pay.
  • Ways of fixing wages in India: law (minimum wages fixed by governments), wage boards, collective bargaining between unions and employers, awards by tribunals, and pay commissions for government staff.
  • The Code on Wages, 2019 now covers minimum wages, payment of wages, bonus and equal pay, and brings in a national floor wage. [CHECK]

7.5 Motivation

Motivation is the inner push that makes a person put effort into work to reach a goal.

Pre-requisites of a sound motivational programme

  • Clear goals of the organisation and of the programme.
  • Understanding what people actually need.
  • Fair and timely rewards, linked to performance.
  • Participation of employees in decisions.
  • Good leadership and open communication.
  • Suitable job design and working conditions.
  • Flexibility, as different people want different things.
  • Regular review of results and cost.

Motivating factors

  • Need hierarchy: people move up from physiological needs (food, pay) to safety (job security), social (belonging), esteem (respect) and self-actualisation (growth).
  • Two-factor view: hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy) only prevent unhappiness. Motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) create real satisfaction.
  • Common factors: fair pay, job security, recognition, promotion chances, good supervision and interesting work.

Morale-building

Morale is the attitude of employees towards their work, colleagues and the organisation. High morale means willingness and teamwork. It is built by fair pay, job security, respect, recognition, participation, quick handling of grievances, good working conditions and open communication. Signs of low morale include high absenteeism, high labour turnover, many grievances, accidents and falling output. A morale survey can measure it.

Example. At Himalaya Handloom, a weaver who made 30 shawls a week earned a fixed wage. The firm began a differential piece rate: a higher rate for every shawl above 30. Output rose, but quality fell. HR then added a quality bonus and a monthly "best weaver" award, giving monetary and non-monetary rewards. Morale improved and rejections dropped.

8. Discipline, grievances, communication and HR records (Unit V)

Discipline means orderly behaviour as per the rules of the organisation. Indiscipline is breaking those rules. Good discipline comes mostly from clear rules, fair treatment and good example, and not just from punishment.

8.1 Causes of disciplinary action

  • Absence without leave and late coming.
  • Disobeying orders (insubordination).
  • Theft, fraud or dishonesty.
  • Violence, abuse or fighting at work.
  • Drunkenness or drugs at work.
  • Negligence that causes damage or accident.
  • Breaking safety rules.
  • Sexual harassment or harassment of colleagues.
  • Giving false information or damaging company property.

Root causes of indiscipline: unclear rules, weak or unfair supervision, poor pay or working conditions, personal stress and weak management.

Steps in disciplinary action

  1. Prepare a preliminary note of the facts.
  2. Issue a charge-sheet (show-cause notice).
  3. Receive the employee's reply.
  4. Hold a fair enquiry. The employee must be given notice and a chance to explain, and the enquiry must be unbiased (natural justice).
  5. Decide on a penalty that fits the fault: warning, fine, suspension, demotion, dismissal.
  6. Allow an appeal and keep a record.

8.2 Model grievance procedure

A grievance is a complaint of an employee about work, pay, treatment or conditions. A grievance procedure is the set of steps to settle it quickly and fairly.

  1. The employee tells the immediate supervisor or department representative, who gives a reply within a short time (about 48 hours). [CHECK]
  2. If not settled, the employee goes to the head of department.
  3. If still not settled, the case goes to the grievance committee, which makes a recommendation.
  4. Management gives its final decision in writing.
  5. The employee can appeal to the chief executive, and the matter may go to voluntary arbitration.

The idea comes from the Code of Discipline model. The Industrial Relations Code, 2020 requires a grievance redressal committee in larger establishments (20 or more workers). Time limits differ by source. [CHECK]

8.4 Maintaining HR records and statistics

  • Records: personal file, attendance, leave, wages, performance, training, discipline, medical and safety.
  • Why: to follow the law, to answer inspections, for planning and decisions, and as proof in a dispute.
  • Good records are accurate, up to date, secure, confidential and easy to find. Many firms now use a human resource information system (HRIS).

HR statistics: useful rates

  • Labour turnover rate = Separations ÷ Average number of employees × 100.
  • Absenteeism rate = Man-days lost ÷ Man-days scheduled × 100.
Example. Himalaya Handloom has an average of 200 employees. During the year 24 left. Turnover = 24 ÷ 200 × 100 = 12%. Scheduled man-days in March: 5,000; days lost: 250. Absenteeism = 250 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 5%. A weaver's complaint about wrongly cut pay reaches the unit head, who corrects it within two days, and a notice on the board tells everyone the new rule.

9.1 Two-mark questions

Define human resource management. (Chapter 1)

The process of getting, developing, motivating and keeping people so that the organisation reaches its goals and employees also grow.

Name the four types of objectives of HRM. (Chapter 1)

Organisational, functional, societal and personal objectives.

What is personnel management? (Chapter 1)

The older and narrower name for managing employees. It mainly handles records, pay, attendance and rules.

What is labour turnover? (Chapter 1)

The rate at which employees leave an organisation. Turnover = Separations ÷ Average employees × 100.

What is workforce diversity? (Chapter 2)

The mix of employees of different age, gender, region, language and background in one organisation.

Name four operative functions of HRM. (Chapter 2)

Procurement, development, compensation and integration. Maintenance and separation are also operative functions.

What is an HR policy? (Chapter 3)

A standing guide that tells managers how to decide HR matters so that similar cases are treated alike.

What is tripartism? (Chapter 3)

Joint talks and decisions among the government, employers and workers on labour matters.

What is HR planning? (Chapter 3)

Forecasting the number and skills of people the organisation will need and planning how to get them at the right time.

What is job analysis? (Chapter 4)

A systematic study of a job to find its duties and the qualities needed to do it.

Distinguish job description from job specification in one line. (Chapter 4)

Job description tells what the job is; job specification tells what kind of person is needed for it.

What is recruitment? (Chapter 4)

Searching for and attracting enough suitable candidates to apply for jobs.

What is orientation? (Chapter 4)

The first introduction of a new employee to the organisation, the job, the team and the rules.

What is vestibule training? (Chapter 5)

Practice on copies of the real machines in a separate room, away from the shop floor.

What is HRD? (Chapter 5)

A planned and continuous effort to build employees' skills, attitudes and motivation, and a culture of trust.

What is 360-degree appraisal? (Chapter 6)

An appraisal in which feedback comes from the boss, peers, subordinates, customers and the employee himself.

What is VRS? (Chapter 6)

Voluntary retirement scheme: an offer that allows employees to retire early, with a payment, by their own choice.

What is a golden handshake? (Chapter 6)

A very generous lump-sum package that encourages senior or well-placed employees to leave early.

What is a fair wage? (Chapter 7)

A wage between the minimum wage and the living wage, based on the firm's ability to pay and the pay for similar work.

What is morale? (Chapter 7)

The attitude of employees towards their work, colleagues and the organisation.

What is a grievance? (Chapter 8)

An employee's complaint about work, pay, treatment or working conditions.

What is the grapevine? (Chapter 8)

The informal network of talk and rumour in an organisation.

What is a charge-sheet? (Chapter 8)

A written notice that states the fault alleged against an employee and asks for a reply.

9.2 Five-mark questions with model answers

Explain the objectives of HRM. (Chapter 1)

Opening: HRM has four groups of objectives that together guide the use of people in an organisation.

  • Organisational: have the right people to reach the goals of the firm.
  • Functional: keep the HR department's service at a level the firm needs, at a fair cost.
  • Societal: act ethically, obey the law and meet the needs of society.
  • Personal: help employees reach their own goals, such as growth and security.

Example: Himalaya Handloom trains weavers (personal), meets orders (organisational), pays fairly and avoids child labour (societal).

Conclusion: Balanced objectives make HRM useful to the firm, the employees and society.

Distinguish between personnel management and HRM. (Chapter 1)

Opening: Personnel management is the older, routine view of managing employees, while HRM is the wider, strategic view.

  • View of people: a cost (personnel) and an asset (HRM).
  • Focus: routine tasks and rules versus long-term growth.
  • Role: support function versus part of top management planning.
  • Style: control and obey versus involve and develop.

Example: A firm that only keeps attendance is practising personnel management. A firm that plans skills for five years ahead is practising HRM.

Conclusion: HRM is a broader and more modern approach.

Explain the challenges faced by a modern HR manager. (Chapter 2)

Opening: A modern HR manager works in a fast-changing environment that brings many pressures.

  • Diversity of the workforce.
  • Globalisation and tough competition for talent.
  • Technology and automation that change skills.
  • High attrition and the need to retain talent.
  • Skill gaps.
  • Changing expectations: work-life balance and flexibility.
  • Changing labour laws. [CHECK]
  • Cost control and ethical issues.

Example: When Himalaya Handloom moved to power looms, the HR manager faced a skill gap, fear of job loss and a need for training at once.

Conclusion: A modern HR manager must be a planner, a trainer and a communicator.

Explain the broad functions of HRM. (Chapter 2)

Opening: HR functions are managerial (planning, organising, directing, controlling) and operative.

  • Procurement: HR planning, recruitment, selection, placement.
  • Development: training, appraisal, career planning.
  • Compensation: pay, incentives, benefits.
  • Integration: motivation, communication, grievance handling.
  • Maintenance: health, safety, welfare.
  • Separation: retirement, resignation, VRS.

Example: A new weaver is recruited (procurement), trained (development), paid (compensation) and finally retires (separation).

Conclusion: The functions together cover an employee's whole career.

Explain the needs and types of HR policies. (Chapter 3)

Opening: An HR policy is a standing guide to decisions on people matters.

  • Needs: consistency, fairness, speed, guidance for new managers, legal compliance, better morale.
  • Types: originated, appealed, imposed, implied, general and specific.
  • A good policy is written, clear, fair, flexible and known to all.

Example: A written leave policy of Himalaya Handloom ensures every weaver gets leave on the same rules.

Conclusion: Policies make HR decisions fair and quick.

Explain the steps in HR planning. (Chapter 3)

Opening: HR planning is forecasting the people needed and planning how to get them.

  • Study the organisation's plans.
  • Forecast the demand for people.
  • Audit the present manpower (supply).
  • Find the gap: shortage or surplus.
  • Prepare the action plan: recruit, train, transfer or VRS.
  • Carry out the plan.
  • Monitor and review.
  • Determinants: business plans, production level, technology, turnover and retirement, skill mix, budget, laws.

Example: The firm needs 60 weavers for a new unit, has 10 to move and so must recruit 50.

Conclusion: HR planning ensures the right people at the right time.

Distinguish between job description and job specification. (Chapter 4)

Opening: Both come from job analysis but answer different questions.

  • Job description: the job (title, duties, reporting, conditions).
  • Job specification: the person (education, skills, experience, traits).
  • Description is used for job design; specification is used in selection.

Example: Description: supervise 20 looms. Specification: textile diploma and 3 years' experience.

Conclusion: Together they help to hire the right person for the right job.

Distinguish between recruitment and selection. (Chapter 4)

Opening: Recruitment attracts candidates, and selection chooses the best of them.

  • Recruitment is positive; selection is negative as it rejects.
  • Recruitment builds a pool; selection picks from the pool.
  • Recruitment ends with applications; selection ends with appointment.
  • Selection involves tests, interviews and medical checks.

Example: An advertisement brings 45 applications (recruitment). A test and interview pick one (selection).

Conclusion: Recruitment must come first, and selection follows it.

Explain the selection process. (Chapter 4)

Opening: Selection is the process of choosing the best candidate from those who applied.

  • Preliminary screening.
  • Application form.
  • Tests.
  • Interview.
  • Reference and background check.
  • Medical examination.
  • Final decision.
  • Offer and appointment letter.

Example: For a loom supervisor, Himalaya Handloom held a skill test and an interview before the offer.

Conclusion: A careful selection saves later cost of turnover and training.

Distinguish between training and development. (Chapter 5)

Opening: Training builds skills for the present job, and development prepares the person for the future.

  • Focus: present job versus future growth.
  • Time: short term versus long term.
  • Who: mostly non-managers versus mostly managers.
  • Nature: skills for a task versus wider growth and attitudes.

Example: Teaching a weaver to run a power loom is training. Preparing a supervisor to become a unit head is development.

Conclusion: Both are needed, as training meets today's needs and development meets tomorrow's.

Explain 360-degree appraisal. (Chapter 6)

Opening: In a 360-degree appraisal, feedback about an employee comes from all around him.

  • Raters: boss, peers, subordinates, customers and self.
  • Advantages: wider and fairer view, less bias, shows strengths and gaps, good for development.
  • Limits: costly and slow, dishonest or friendly ratings, needs trust and training of raters.

Example: A supervisor at Himalaya Handloom is rated by the unit head, other supervisors, his weavers and a dealer.

Conclusion: It gives a balanced view but works well only in a climate of trust.

Explain VRS and golden handshake. (Chapter 6)

Opening: Both are ways of early exit with a payment.

  • VRS: employees choose to retire early, with a payment. Used to reduce surplus staff without forced exits.
  • Golden handshake: a very generous lump sum, usually for senior or well-placed people.
  • Both should be fair and open to all in the group.
  • Tax rules on the payment change. [CHECK]

Example: Himalaya Handloom offered a VRS to 15 surplus hand-loom workers and nine accepted.

Conclusion: These schemes reduce staff with less conflict than forced exits, but may cause good people to leave.

Explain the factors affecting compensation policy. (Chapter 7)

Opening: Compensation policy is the plan for paying employees. Several factors shape it.

  • Ability to pay.
  • Demand and supply of labour.
  • Cost of living.
  • Productivity.
  • Trade union strength.
  • Government laws and minimum wage rules.
  • Job evaluation and prevailing pay.

Example: A weaver's pay at Himalaya Handloom depends on the firm's income, the pay in nearby units and the legal minimum wage.

Conclusion: A good policy balances the firm's means and the employee's needs.

Explain the pre-requisites of a sound motivational programme. (Chapter 7)

Opening: A motivational programme works only if certain conditions are met.

  • Clear goals.
  • Knowing the needs of employees.
  • Fair and timely rewards linked to performance.
  • Participation of employees.
  • Good leadership and open communication.
  • Suitable job design.
  • Flexibility.
  • Review of results and cost.

Example: The quality bonus and best weaver award at Himalaya Handloom met several of these conditions.

Conclusion: Motivation needs planning and cannot rely on money alone.

Explain the model grievance procedure. (Chapter 8)

Opening: A grievance procedure is a set of steps for settling complaints quickly and fairly.

  • Employee tells the supervisor or department representative, who replies within a short time. [CHECK]
  • If unsettled, the department head decides.
  • Then the grievance committee gives a recommendation.
  • Management gives a written decision.
  • Employee may appeal to the chief executive; voluntary arbitration is possible.

Example: A weaver complains about a wrong pay cut. The unit head corrects it in two days.

Conclusion: A clear procedure reduces anger and keeps morale high.

Explain the channels of communication. (Chapter 8)

Opening: Communication is passing information and understanding between people in an organisation.

  • Downward: orders, policies.
  • Upward: suggestions, reports, complaints.
  • Horizontal: between equals.
  • Diagonal: across levels and departments.
  • Informal (grapevine): talk and rumour; fast but may be wrong.

Example: A circular about a new shift is downward; a weaver's suggestion is upward.

Conclusion: A good organisation uses all channels and corrects rumours with facts.

9.3 Ten-mark questions with model answers

Define HRM. Explain its objectives and importance. (Chapter 1)

Opening: HRM is the process of acquiring, developing, motivating and maintaining people so that organisational goals are reached.

  • Nature: people-focused, continuous and pervasive.
  • Organisational objective: right people for the goals.
  • Functional objective: HR service at a suitable level and cost.
  • Societal objective: ethics and legal duties.
  • Personal objective: growth and security of employees.
  • Importance for the firm: productivity, low turnover, good culture.
  • For employees: fair pay, training, job satisfaction.
  • For society: jobs, skills, industrial peace.

Example: At Himalaya Handloom, pay bands and training reduced the number of weavers leaving and raised output.

Conclusion: People are the only resource that can learn and improve, so HRM is central to success.

Explain the challenges of a modern HR manager and the broad HR functions. (Chapter 2)

Opening: A modern HR manager must meet many challenges using several functions.

  • Challenges: diversity, globalisation, technology, attrition, skill gap, changing expectations, legal change, cost control, ethics.
  • Managerial functions: planning, organising, directing, controlling.
  • Operative functions: procurement, development, compensation, integration, maintenance, separation.
  • The challenges are met by using the functions together.

Example: A shift to power looms required planning (how many operators), training, fair pay and open communication.

Conclusion: The HR manager acts as planner, trainer and communicator.

Explain HR planning: its steps and the determinants of HR requirements. (Chapter 3)

Opening: HR planning forecasts the people needed and plans how to get them.

  • Steps: study plans; forecast demand; audit supply; find the gap; make the action plan; implement; review.
  • Determinants: business growth, production level, technology, turnover and retirement, absenteeism, skill mix, budget, government rules, nature of industry.
  • The action plan may use recruitment, training, transfer or VRS.

Example: A second unit needs 60 weavers and 4 supervisors; 50 weavers and 1 supervisor must be recruited.

Conclusion: Good planning prevents both shortages and surplus.

Discuss Indian labour policy. (Chapter 3)

Opening: Indian labour policy is the set of government principles and laws that protect workers and keep industrial peace.

  • Based on the Constitution's Directive Principles.
  • Tripartism: government, employers and workers decide together.
  • Aims: industrial peace, welfare, social security, fair wages, productivity.
  • Earlier tools: Five-Year Plans, ILO standards, Code of Discipline. [CHECK]
  • Recent change: four labour codes (Wages, Industrial Relations, Social Security, Occupational Safety and Health) merged 29 central laws; in force from 21 November 2025; Central Rules notified 8 May 2026; state rules still coming. [CHECK]

Example: A firm now follows the Code on Wages for pay and the Industrial Relations Code for standing orders and grievance committees.

Conclusion: The policy is moving to a simpler set of laws, and HR managers must keep their rules updated.

Describe the recruitment and selection process. (Chapter 4)

Opening: Recruitment attracts candidates and selection chooses the best of them.

  • Recruitment sources: internal (promotion, transfer, referrals) and external (advertisements, campus, agencies, portals).
  • Recruitment steps: vacancy, job description and specification, choose source, invite applications, screen.
  • Selection steps: screening, application form, tests, interview, reference check, medical, final decision, offer.
  • Then placement and orientation.

Example: Himalaya Handloom received 45 applications for a supervisor post, shortlisted 12 and appointed one after tests and interviews.

Conclusion: A careful process saves the cost of wrong hiring.

Explain the methods of training and how training is evaluated. (Chapter 5)

Opening: Training methods are of two groups: on-the-job and off-the-job.

  • On-the-job: coaching, job rotation, understudy, apprenticeship.
  • Off-the-job: lectures, case studies, role play, simulation, conferences, e-learning, vestibule training.
  • Evaluation: reaction, learning, behaviour, results.
  • Choose the method by cost, number of trainees and the skill needed.

Example: Operators learn power looms by coaching; supervisors attend a workshop. After three months output and quality are checked.

Conclusion: Evaluation shows whether the money spent on training was worth it.

Explain performance appraisal methods, including 360-degree appraisal. (Chapter 6)

Opening: Performance appraisal evaluates an employee's work and potential against standards.

  • Traditional: ranking, paired comparison, grading, rating scale, checklist, forced distribution, critical incident, essay.
  • Modern: MBO, BARS, assessment centre, 360-degree.
  • 360-degree: feedback from boss, peers, subordinates, customers, self; fair but costly and needs trust.
  • Errors: halo, leniency, central tendency, bias, recency.

Example: A supervisor is rated by the unit head, other supervisors, his weavers and a dealer, and a feedback meeting follows.

Conclusion: The method should suit the purpose, and raters should be trained to avoid errors.

Explain incentive systems of payment and wage fixation in India. (Chapter 7)

Opening: An incentive is extra pay linked to performance, and wage fixation is how pay levels are set.

  • Individual incentives: piece rate, differential piece rate, time bonus, commission.
  • Group: group bonus. Organisation-wide: profit sharing, gain sharing, stock options.
  • Wage concepts: minimum, fair and living wage.
  • Fixation: law, wage boards, collective bargaining, tribunal awards, pay commissions.
  • Code on Wages, 2019 covers minimum wages and a national floor wage. [CHECK]

Example: A differential piece rate lifted output at Himalaya Handloom, and a quality bonus protected quality.

Conclusion: Good incentives are simple, fair and linked to quality, and wages must meet legal minimums.

Explain motivation: motivating factors and morale-building. (Chapter 7)

Opening: Motivation is the inner drive to put effort into work to reach a goal.

  • Need hierarchy: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation.
  • Two-factor view: hygiene factors prevent unhappiness; motivators create satisfaction.
  • Morale-building: fair pay, job security, respect, participation, grievance handling, good conditions.
  • Signs of low morale: absenteeism, turnover, grievances, accidents.

Example: A best weaver award and a quality bonus raised morale at Himalaya Handloom.

Conclusion: Morale is lasting only when both pay and recognition are fair.

Explain the causes and procedure of disciplinary action and the model grievance procedure. (Chapter 8)

Opening: Discipline is orderly behaviour as per rules, and a grievance procedure settles complaints.

  • Causes: absence, insubordination, theft, violence, drunkenness, negligence, safety violations, harassment.
  • Procedure: facts, charge-sheet, reply, fair enquiry, penalty, appeal.
  • Penalties: warning, fine, suspension, demotion, dismissal.
  • Grievance steps: supervisor, department head, grievance committee, management decision, appeal and arbitration. [CHECK]

Example: A weaver absent for five days gets a charge-sheet, a reply and an enquiry before a warning is issued.

Conclusion: Fair procedure, natural justice and records protect both employee and employer.

Remember this: Theory answers need a clear opening, headed points, an example from everyday work and a short conclusion. Write the names of the steps, types and channels as headings.

12. Exam tips

Time management

  • Read the whole paper for 10 minutes and tick the questions you know best.
  • Give time in proportion to marks. As a guide, spend about 3 minutes on a 2-mark answer, 8 minutes on a 5-mark answer and 18 to 20 minutes on a 10-mark answer. Change this to suit your paper.
  • Start with your strongest question to settle your nerves.
  • Keep the last 10 minutes to check headings and numbering.

How to write 5-mark and 10-mark answers

  • Use this pattern: definition or opening line, main points, example, conclusion.
  • Write each point as a short heading with one or two lines of explanation.
  • Number the steps and keep them in order (HR planning, selection, grievance procedure).
  • Add a small table or flow chart where it helps, such as the recruitment and selection process.
  • Use an example from a real workplace, such as a factory, shop or office.

What examiners look for

  • Correct definitions in your own words.
  • All the points the question asks for (for "types", "steps" or "factors", give the full list).
  • Clear headings and neat layout.
  • Examples and up-to-date points, such as the new labour codes. [CHECK]

Common errors in this subject

  • Mixing up job description and job specification.
  • Mixing up recruitment and selection, or training and development.
  • Writing long paragraphs with no headings.
  • Missing one or two steps in a process.
  • Giving points without any example.
  • Using old law names without checking the current position. [CHECK]

Q1. Which of these is NOT an objective of HRM?
(a) Organisational
(b) Functional
(c) Societal
(d) Mechanical

Answer: (d) Mechanical. The four objectives are organisational, functional, societal and personal.

Q2. Compared with personnel management, HRM treats people mainly as:
(a) A cost
(b) An asset
(c) A tool
(d) A risk

Answer: (b) An asset. HRM sees people as an asset to be developed.

Q3. 30 employees left in a year. The average number of employees was 300. Labour turnover is:
(a) 10%
(b) 5%
(c) 30%
(d) 1%

Answer: (a) 10%. 30 ÷ 300 × 100 = 10%.

Q4. Which is an operative function of HRM?
(a) Planning
(b) Organising
(c) Compensation
(d) Controlling

Answer: (c) Compensation. Planning, organising and controlling are managerial functions.

Q5. A policy forced on a firm by law or a trade union is called:
(a) Originated
(b) Appealed
(c) Imposed
(d) Implied

Answer: (c) Imposed. Imposed policies come from outside the firm.

Q6. The first step in HR planning is to:
(a) Study the plans of the organisation
(b) Recruit staff
(c) Train staff
(d) Offer VRS

Answer: (a) Study the plans of the organisation. Demand for people comes from the organisation's plans.

Q7. A job specification describes:
(a) Duties of the job
(b) Qualities needed in the job holder
(c) Pay of the job
(d) Working hours

Answer: (b) Qualities needed in the job holder. Description covers the job; specification covers the person.

Q8. Recruitment is called a positive process because it:
(a) Attracts candidates
(b) Rejects candidates
(c) Trains candidates
(d) Pays candidates

Answer: (a) Attracts candidates. Selection is negative as it rejects the unfit.

Q9. Putting a selected person on the job that suits him is called:
(a) Recruitment
(b) Placement
(c) Appraisal
(d) Retrenchment

Answer: (b) Placement. Placement matches the person and the job.

Q10. Vestibule training means:
(a) Practice on copies of machines away from the shop floor
(b) Learning from the boss at the workplace
(c) Reading a manual at home
(d) A short motivational talk

Answer: (a) Practice on copies of machines away from the shop floor. It is an off-the-job method using dummy equipment.

Q11. Which is an on-the-job method of training?
(a) Role play
(b) Case study
(c) Job rotation
(d) Lecture

Answer: (c) Job rotation. Job rotation takes place at the workplace.

Q12. In the four-level view of training evaluation, "Did the trainees learn?" is the level of:
(a) Reaction
(b) Learning
(c) Behaviour
(d) Results

Answer: (b) Learning. Learning is checked by tests before and after training.

Q13. 360-degree appraisal takes feedback from:
(a) The boss only
(b) Boss, peers, subordinates, customers and self
(c) Customers only
(d) The employee only

Answer: (b) Boss, peers, subordinates, customers and self. It uses feedback from all around the employee.

Q14. When one good trait of an employee influences all his ratings, the error is called:
(a) Halo effect
(b) Leniency
(c) Central tendency
(d) Recency

Answer: (a) Halo effect. This is the halo effect.

Q15. VRS is:
(a) An employer's forced layoff

(d) A type of transfer

Answer: (b) An offer of early retirement with payment by employee's choice. VRS is voluntary and carries a payment.

Q16. A wage that lies between the minimum and the living wage is called:
(a) Minimum wage
(b) Fair wage
(c) Living wage
(d) Real wage

Answer: (b) Fair wage. This is the fair wage.

Q17. Hygiene factors, in the two-factor view, mainly:
(a) Create strong satisfaction
(b) Prevent dissatisfaction
(c) Have no effect
(d) Are always non-monetary

Answer: (b) Prevent dissatisfaction. They stop unhappiness but do not create real motivation.

Q18. Which of these is a group incentive?
(a) Piece rate
(b) Commission
(c) Group bonus

Q19. A suggestion from a worker to the manager is an example of:

(b) Upward communication

Q20. Giving an employee a fair notice and a fair hearing before punishment is called:
(a) Natural justice
