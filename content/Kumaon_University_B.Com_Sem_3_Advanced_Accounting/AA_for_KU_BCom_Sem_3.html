I

Insurance claims and investment accounts

1.1 Claim for loss of stock

A fire insurance policy pays for the loss of stock up to the policy amount. First find the stock on the date of fire, then the loss, then the claim.

  • Make a memorandum trading account from the last balancing date to the date of fire. It is only a working, not a ledger account.
  • Cost of goods sold = Sales − Gross profit (or Sales × (100 − GP%) ÷ 100).
  • Stock at fire = Opening stock + Purchases − Cost of goods sold. Adjust for goods saved (salvage), goods on approval and abnormal items given in the question.
  • Loss = Stock at fire − Salvage.
  • Average clause: if policy amount is less than stock at fire, claim = Loss × Policy ÷ Stock at fire. Claim never exceeds the policy.
Rawat Traders. A fire destroys stock worth ₹90,000 (stock at fire). Salvage is nil. The shop's policy is only ₹60,000. Loss ₹90,000. Claim = 90,000 × 60,000 ÷ 90,000 = ₹60,000. In a second case the loss is ₹30,000: claim = 30,000 × 60/90 = ₹20,000.

1.2 Claim for loss of profit (consequential loss)

  • This policy covers the fall in profit while the business is hit. It pays for short sales and extra running cost.
  • Short sales = Standard turnover − Actual turnover in the indemnity period (the time the policy covers).
  • Basic claim = Short sales × Rate of gross profit. Rate = (Gross profit ÷ Turnover of the last year) × 100. For this policy, gross profit = net profit + insured standing charges.
  • Add increased cost of working (extra spending to save sales), within the limit in the policy. Books differ on the exact limit and its link with the average clause [CHECK in your prescribed book].
  • Average clause: multiply the claim by Policy ÷ (Annual turnover × Rate of GP).
Remember this: Stock claim = Loss × Policy ÷ Stock at fire. Profit claim = Short sales × GP rate (then average clause).

Common mistake: using sales instead of cost of goods sold when finding stock, and forgetting salvage.

1.3 Investment accounts: cum-interest and ex-interest

An investment account records buying and selling of shares or debentures. The price may be quoted in two ways.

Cum-interestEx-interest
Price includesInterest accrued up to purchase dateNo accrued interest; seller keeps it
Buyer paysPrice (includes interest)Price + accrued interest separately
Cost of investmentPrice − accrued interestPrice
  • Interest is worked on nominal value for the months held. Accrued interest is income, not cost. Show it in the interest column.
  • Profit or loss on sale excludes accrued interest. At the year end, closing investments are valued at lower of cost or market value.
  • For shares, the same idea is called cum-dividend and ex-dividend. Dividend declared before purchase is a capital receipt and reduces cost.
Rawat Traders buys 12% debentures of ₹10,000 nominal on 1 Oct at ₹10,300 cum-interest. Interest is paid on 30 June and 31 Dec. Accrued interest for 3 months = 10,000 × 12% × 3/12 = ₹300. Cost = 10,300 − 300 = ₹10,000.

Common mistake: adding accrued interest to cost, or counting interest on the price instead of nominal value.

II

Final accounts of banks and insurance companies

2.1 Banking companies

  • Banks follow the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, Third Schedule. Form A is the balance sheet (Schedules 1 to 11). Form B is the profit and loss account (Schedules 12 to 16).
  • Form A: capital, reserves, deposits, borrowings, other liabilities; cash with RBI, balances with banks, investments, advances, fixed assets, other assets.
  • Form B: interest earned, other income, interest expended, operating expenses, provisions.
  • Rebate on bills discounted is interest of the next year. It is income not yet earned, so show it under other liabilities.
  • Non-performing assets (NPA) are loans where interest or instalment is overdue. They are graded as sub-standard, doubtful and loss; provisions rise with the grade. Interest on NPAs is not counted as income until received. Provision rates change with RBI circulars [CHECK current rates].
  • Out of net profit, at least 20% goes to the statutory reserve (Section 17).

2.2 Insurance companies

  • Rules come from the Insurance Act, 1938 and IRDA regulations. Life insurers prepare a revenue account, profit and loss account and balance sheet. General insurers prepare a separate revenue account for fire, marine and miscellaneous business.
  • Life: premiums, commission, claims (death and maturity), and the life fund, which is the surplus kept to meet future policy payments.
  • General: claims incurred = claims paid + closing outstanding − opening outstanding. A reserve for unexpired risk covers the part of premium for the period after the year end. Rates differ by class (often 50% for fire and miscellaneous, 100% for marine) [CHECK].
Rawat Traders insures its shop with a general insurer. The premium covers 12 months from 1 Oct. At 31 March, 6 months of cover remain. That half of the premium is an unexpired risk, not this year's income.
Remember this: Form A = A for assets and liabilities (balance sheet). Form B = B for business results (profit and loss).

Common mistake: treating rebate on bills discounted as income of this year, and mixing up Form A and Form B.

III

Valuation of goodwill and shares

3.1 Goodwill

Goodwill is the extra value of a business above its net assets. It comes from good name, location and loyal customers. It is an intangible asset; it cannot be seen but has value. We value it when a partner joins or leaves, on sale or merger, or for tax and share value.

MethodFormula
Average profitAverage profit × years of purchase
Super profitSuper profit × years of purchase. Super profit = Average profit − Normal profit. Normal profit = Capital employed × Normal rate
Capitalisation of average profitAverage profit × 100 ÷ Normal rate − Capital employed
Capitalisation of super profitSuper profit × 100 ÷ Normal rate
AnnuitySuper profit × present value factor of the annuity

Capital employed = Assets (without goodwill) − Outside liabilities. Books differ on whether to use opening or average capital [CHECK].

Rawat Traders: profits of 3 years were ₹60,000, ₹80,000 and ₹70,000. Average = ₹70,000. Capital employed = ₹5,00,000; normal rate 10%, so normal profit = ₹50,000. Super profit = ₹20,000. Goodwill at 3 years' purchase of super profit = ₹60,000. By capitalising average profit: 70,000 × 100/10 = 7,00,000; less 5,00,000 = ₹2,00,000. By capitalising super profit: 20,000 × 100/10 = ₹2,00,000. The two capitalisation methods give the same answer.

3.2 Shares

Valuation of shares is needed for tax, mergers, loans against shares and buying out holders. There are three main methods.

  • Net assets (intrinsic value): (Assets − Outside liabilities − Preference capital) ÷ Number of equity shares. Add goodwill and use real worth of assets.
  • Yield (earning) method: Value = (Expected rate ÷ Normal rate) × Paid-up value per share. Expected rate = Profit for equity ÷ Equity capital × 100.
  • Fair value: average of net assets value and yield value.
Remember this: Super profit = Average − Normal. Intrinsic value = Net assets for equity ÷ Equity shares.

Common mistake: forgetting to deduct preference capital, and using total profit instead of profit for equity.

IV

Liquidation of companies

Liquidation (winding up) ends a company's life: assets are sold, debts paid, and any balance goes to members. A liquidator does this work.

  • Modes under the Companies Act: by the Tribunal, or voluntary (by members or by creditors). Insolvent companies are also dealt with under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code [CHECK how your book treats this].
  • Statement of affairs shows assets at estimated realisable value and liabilities, and the surplus or deficiency for members. The deficiency account explains how the deficiency arose.
  • Liquidator's final statement of account is a receipts and payments account.
  • Order of payment: secured creditors from their security; liquidation costs; preferential creditors (such as workers' dues and taxes); unsecured creditors; preference shareholders; equity shareholders. The exact rank of some dues differs between old and new law [CHECK].
  • Unpaid calls on partly paid shares are collected from contributories (shareholders liable to pay) and added to receipts.
  • Liquidator's remuneration is usually a percentage of assets realised or of payments made. Use the rate given in the question.
Rawat Traders Ltd goes into voluntary liquidation. The shop and stock sell for ₹8 lakh. After paying liquidation costs and workers' dues, the balance goes first to the unsecured suppliers. Shareholders get something only if money is left.
Remember this: Secured, costs, preferential, unsecured, preference shares, equity shares: owners are always paid last.

Common mistake: paying shareholders before unsecured creditors, and forgetting unpaid calls.

V

Amalgamation of companies (AS 14)

Amalgamation is joining of two or more companies. The one that disappears is the transferor; the one that takes over is the transferee. AS 14 is the standard named in your syllabus. Companies that follow Ind AS use a different standard, so check with your teacher [CHECK].

5.1 Two types

In the nature of mergerIn the nature of purchase
TestAll five conditions are metAny condition fails
MethodPooling of interestsPurchase method
ReservesCarried forward as they areNot carried (except statutory reserves)
DifferenceAdjusted in reservesGoodwill or capital reserve

Five conditions for merger: (1) all assets and liabilities pass to the transferee; (2) holders of at least 90% of the equity (by face value) of the transferor become equity holders of the transferee; (3) the price is paid in equity shares, with cash only for fractions; (4) the business will continue; (5) assets and liabilities are kept at book value, apart from changes for uniform policy.

5.2 Purchase consideration

  • Purchase consideration is what the transferee gives to the transferor's shareholders (shares, cash, debentures).
  • Net assets method: Agreed value of assets taken over − liabilities taken over. Lump sum: a fixed amount. Intrinsic value (share exchange): shares issued on the basis of the value per share of both companies.
  • Purchase method: if consideration > net assets, the extra is goodwill. If less, the difference is capital reserve.
Rawat Traders Ltd takes over Negi Stores Ltd (made-up). Negi's assets are ₹10 lakh and liabilities ₹2 lakh, so net assets are ₹8 lakh. Rawat pays ₹9 lakh in shares. The extra ₹1 lakh is goodwill (purchase method).
Remember this: Merger = 5 conditions + pooling. Fail any one = purchase. Consideration > net assets = goodwill.

Common mistake: adding assets not taken over, and treating a fresh capital reserve in a merger.
