I

Nature and analysis of business environment

Business environment means all the forces outside and inside a firm that affect how it works. Some forces are controllable (inside); others are not (outside).

  • Nature: it is dynamic (always changing), complex, uncertain, relative (differs by place) and has long-term effects.
  • Dimensions: economic, political, legal, social and cultural, technological, natural and global. These make up the macro environment. The micro environment is suppliers, customers, competitors and the public.
  • Analysis is studying the environment to find opportunities and threats and to match them with the firm's strengths and weaknesses.

1.1 Framework of analysis

  • PESTLE: list political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors.
  • SWOT: strengths and weaknesses (inside), opportunities and threats (outside).
  • ETOP (environmental threat and opportunity profile): a table that rates each factor as a threat or an opportunity. Books may use different frameworks [CHECK your teacher's list].

1.2 Scanning, monitoring, forecasting, assessment

StepPlain meaning
ScanningLooking widely for early signals of change
MonitoringTracking the signals already spotted
ForecastingEstimating what may happen next
AssessmentJudging what it means for the firm and what to do
Doon Organics. A new rule on food labels is announced (scanning). The firm follows the draft rules (monitoring), estimates a rise in packing cost (forecasting) and decides to change its labels (assessment).
Remember this: Scan, monitor, forecast, assess: see it, follow it, project it, act on it.

Common mistake: treating scanning and monitoring as the same, and calling a threat a weakness (weakness is internal).

II

India's economic environment

Economic environment covers the nature of the economy, its policies and the conditions that affect demand, cost and profit.

2.1 Growth strategy

  • India chose a mixed economy: public and private sectors together, with planning. Five-Year Plans guided growth. Early plans stressed heavy industry and self-reliance (import substitution).
  • The Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog in 2015.

2.2 Assessing the economic environment

  • Look at indicators: GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, fiscal deficit, exchange rate, unemployment and per capita income.
  • Economic markets: money market (short-term funds), capital market (long-term funds, shares and debentures), foreign exchange market and commodity market. Your syllabus wording is brief [CHECK what your teacher includes].

2.3 Economic reforms and their consequences

  • In 1991, India faced a foreign exchange crisis and started reforms: delicensing, lower import duties, easier foreign investment, and a reduced public sector role.
  • Positive effects: faster growth, more competition, a strong services and IT sector, more choice for buyers.
  • Criticisms: uneven gains between regions and classes, slow job growth in industry, and pressure on small firms. Economists debate how far reforms caused each effect.
Doon Organics. After 1991, a Dehradun firm no longer needs a licence to start or expand. It now also faces imported spices, so it must improve quality and price.
Remember this: LPG: Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation (1991).

Common mistake: mixing up Planning Commission and NITI Aayog, and listing only the benefits of reforms.

III

Technological environment

Technology is the methods, tools and knowledge used to make or sell things. Technological environment means the changes in technology around a firm.

3.1 Technology and business

  • Technology lowers cost, improves quality, creates new products, and can make old products or jobs obsolete.
  • Firms need research and development (R&D) or must buy technology from others.

3.2 Management of technology transfer

Technology transfer means passing know-how from one firm or country to another. Routes are shown above. The manager must choose the right technology, check its cost and fit, train people, and make sure the firm can absorb and adapt it.

3.3 Analysis, forecasting and assessment

  • Analysis: study current technology in the industry and the rivals' level.
  • Forecasting: trend projection, expert opinion, the Delphi method (repeated rounds of expert opinion) and scenarios.
  • Assessment: judge cost, risk, benefit and effect on staff before adopting.
Doon Organics. Doon Organics buys a packing machine on licence from a foreign maker. The maker trains two staff. Later the firm modifies the machine for small pouches. Adapting is the sign of real absorption.
Remember this: Buy it, fit it, train for it, adapt it.

Common mistake: thinking technology transfer is only buying machines; know-how and training count too.

IV

Global environment

4.1 Why firms go global

  • Larger markets and more sales; spreading risk across countries.
  • Lower cost of labour, raw material or production.
  • Gaining scale, new technology and a global brand.
  • Home market is saturated, or the government gives incentives.

4.2 Routes of globalisation

  • Exporting: make at home, sell abroad. Licensing and franchising: let a foreign firm use your brand or technology for a fee. Joint venture: share ownership with a foreign partner. Wholly owned subsidiary: own unit abroad.

4.3 Active players in global business

Multinational corporations (MNCs), national governments, regional trade blocs and global bodies such as the WTO, IMF and World Bank. Books group the players differently [CHECK].

4.4 FDI: India's experience

  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) is money put by a foreign firm into a business in India with a lasting interest and control.
  • Before 1991 India was cautious. After reforms most sectors opened, through the automatic route (no prior approval) or the government route (approval needed). Sector limits change often [CHECK current rules].
  • Benefits: capital, technology, jobs, exports. Concerns: pressure on local firms, profit sent abroad, control in foreign hands.

4.5 WTO: benefits and problems for India

  • The World Trade Organisation started on 1 January 1995, replacing GATT. It sets trade rules, settles disputes and is based in Geneva. India is a founding member.
  • Benefits: wider market access for exports, rules that are the same for all, and a dispute settlement system.
  • Problems: costlier patents (TRIPS), farm subsidy disputes with rich countries, non-tariff barriers, and more competition from imports. Views differ on net gain.
Doon Organics. A Dehradun firm exports millet flour (exporting), then lets a Dubai trader use its brand (licensing). If sales grow, it may open its own packing unit there (subsidiary).
Remember this: Export, licence, joint venture, own unit: more control, more risk.

Common mistake: mixing FDI (controlling stake) with portfolio investment (shares only), and forgetting that WTO replaced GATT.

V

Political and legal environment: MRTP Act and FEMA

The political and legal environment is the government's policies and laws that guide business. This unit names two laws.

5.1 MRTP Act, 1969

  • Aim: stop concentration of economic power, and control monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices.
  • Monopolistic trade practice: misuse of dominant position, for example raising prices unfairly. Restrictive trade practice: a practice that blocks competition, such as tie-in sales. Unfair trade practice: false claims or misleading ads.
  • The MRTP Commission heard complaints and could order a firm to stop.
  • After 1991, controls on size and expansion were removed. The Act was later replaced by the Competition Act, 2002 and the Competition Commission of India [CHECK if your paper still asks the old Act].

5.2 FEMA, 1999

  • The Foreign Exchange Management Act replaced FERA, 1973. It came into force in 2000.
  • Aim: help external trade and payments, and the orderly growth of the foreign exchange market.
  • It is a civil law; FERA was criminal and strict. Current account deals (such as import payments) are generally free; capital account deals are regulated. The RBI manages it through authorised dealers.
  • Breaches lead to penalties, with the Enforcement Directorate investigating.
Doon Organics. Doon Organics buys packing material from abroad (current account, free) but wants to invest in a foreign firm (capital account, regulated by RBI rules). It also advertises truthfully to avoid unfair trade practice charges.
Remember this: FERA = fear, strict; FEMA = manage, facilitate. MRTP = curb monopoly.

Common mistake: saying FEMA is criminal, and saying MRTP is the current law without noting the Competition Act.

Exam tips

  • Time: about 1 mark per minute; start with the questions you know best.
  • 5 marks: definition, 4 headed points, one line example.
  • 8 marks: definition, headed points, table or diagram, example, conclusion.
  • Examiners look for: correct terms, years of laws, and balance (benefits and problems).
  • Common errors: mixing FERA and FEMA, forgetting the Competition Act, giving only benefits of reforms or WTO.
