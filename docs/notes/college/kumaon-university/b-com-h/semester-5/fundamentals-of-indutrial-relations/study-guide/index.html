1. Meaning, scope, objectives and utility of industrial relations (Unit I)

Industrial relations (IR) means the relationships among employers, employees, their unions and the State, which arise from work in industry. It is about the rules and habits that decide how work is organised, how pay is fixed and how quarrels are settled.

1.1 Quick notes

  • Systems view: IR is a system with three actors: managers, workers with their organisations, and government agencies. They work within a setting of technology, markets and power, and the result is a set of workplace rules.
  • Scope: labour relations (bargaining, disputes), employer-employee policies (pay, discipline, welfare), the role of government, unions and employers' bodies, dispute machinery, and workers' participation.
  • Objectives: peace and understanding between the parties; fewer strikes and lock-outs; higher output; respect for labour law; better life for workers; industrial democracy.

1.2 Utility of studying IR

ForMain points
EmployerIndustrial peace; higher output; less absenteeism and turnover; knowledge of legal duties; better HR policies
WorkerKnows rights; uses unions and grievance steps well; gets a voice; understands management limits
SocietySteady supply of goods; lower social cost of conflict; fairer sharing of gains
Shivalik Garments. The factory has 400 workers, a union, a Works Committee and a set of standing orders. All three actors meet here: the owner and supervisors (management), the union office-bearers, and the labour officer (government).
Remember this. IR = employer + employee + union + State. Dunlop: three actors, one set of rules.
Common mistakes. Writing that IR is only about strikes. It also covers cooperation, rules and welfare.

2. Approaches to industrial relations (Unit I)

An approach is a way of looking at the employment relationship. The three classic ones differ on one question: is a conflict of interest between employer and worker normal?

2.1 Quick notes

  • Unitary: the firm is one team with one authority, management. Conflict is abnormal and comes from poor communication or troublemakers. Unions are seen as outsiders.
  • Pluralist: the firm is a group of interests (managers, workers, owners), each with a fair claim. Conflict is normal and can be managed through bargaining and rules. Unions are legitimate. Indian dispute law follows this view.
  • Marxist (radical): conflict comes from the divide between owners of capital and workers. Unions are tools of class struggle. Real peace needs a change in ownership and control.
BasisUnitaryPluralistMarxist
OrganisationOne teamCoalition of groupsClass arena
ConflictAbnormalInevitable, manageableStructural
UnionsIntrusionLegitimateInstrument of struggle
RemedyCommunication, HR careBargaining, lawChange in ownership
Shivalik Garments. A manager at the factory says a strike was caused by agitators from outside. That is the unitary view. If the owner instead meets the union and signs an agreement, that is the pluralist view.
Remember this. Unitary = one team. Pluralist = many groups. Marxist = two classes.
Common mistakes. Saying the pluralist view wants to remove conflict. It wants to manage conflict, not remove it.

3.3 Shop-floor IR

  • It is the daily relationship at the work place. The supervisor is the first and closest face of management.
  • Small issues should be settled informally here, before they become formal grievances or disputes.
  • Works Committees and a step-by-step grievance procedure support it (see Chapters 5 and 7).
  • Good shop-floor IR prevents strikes, builds trust and warns management early. It needs trained, fair supervisors.

Remember this. Policy is made at the top; peace is made at the shop floor.
Common mistakes. Giving only government roles, or forgetting the supervisor in shop-floor IR.

4. Standing orders (Unit I)

Standing orders are written rules on service conditions (classification, hours, leave, discipline, termination) that an establishment must frame, get certified and display. The law is the Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, 1946, where Sec. 2(g) defines them.

4.1 Quick notes

  • Objects: make service terms clear and uniform, reduce friction, and give workers certainty.
  • Applies to: establishments with 100 or more workmen. State governments may extend it to smaller ones. The IR Code 2020 raises the limit to 300 [CHECK].
  • Schedule matters (examples): classes of workmen; hours, holidays and pay days; shifts; attendance; leave; gate entry and search; stoppages and closing of sections; termination and notice; suspension and dismissal for misconduct; redress against unfair treatment.
  • Modification: again needs certification; normally not within six months of the last change, except by agreement.
  • Importance: certainty; prevents disputes; a fair base for discipline and natural justice; used in Labour Court cases.

4.2 Certification procedure

  1. Employer sends five copies of the draft, with details of workmen and the union, to the Certifying Officer. The draft should follow the model standing orders as far as practicable.
  2. The officer sends a copy to the union (or workmen) for objections.
  3. After hearing both sides, the officer certifies the orders, with changes if needed.
  4. An aggrieved party appeals to the Appellate Authority within 30 days.
  5. The orders take effect 30 days after authenticated copies are sent (or from the appeal order) and must be displayed in the workers' language.

4.3 Certified vs model standing orders

BasisCertifiedModel
Framed byThe employer, for one establishmentThe government, in general form
ProcessDraft, objections, hearing, certificationNone; apply by notification
AppliesTo that establishment once certifiedUntil certified orders start, and where they are silent

Shivalik Garments. The factory frames its own orders on termination notice. Once certified, they bind it. Before that, and on any matter they do not cover, the model orders apply.
Remember this. Draft, objections, hearing, certify, appeal in 30 days.

5. Industrial unrest, disputes and workers' participation (Unit II)

5.1 Unrest and disputes

  • Industrial unrest is a general mood of dissatisfaction that may or may not become a formal quarrel. It is not defined in the Act.
  • Industrial dispute is a formal difference defined in Sec. 2(k) of the ID Act, connected with employment, non-employment, or terms and conditions of labour.
CauseExamples
EconomicWages, bonus, allowances, hours, leave
ManagerialUnfair dismissal, victimisation, poor supervision, weak communication
PoliticalInter-union rivalry, outside political influence
LegalDifferent readings of standing orders, awards or agreements
  • Prevention: fair pay and promotion, clear standing orders, grievance procedure, joint consultation, responsible unions, respect for the Code of Discipline of 1958 [CHECK].
  • Settlement: bargaining, conciliation, voluntary arbitration, then adjudication by labour courts or tribunals.
Shivalik Garments. Workers at the factory expect a bonus. Management announces a cut. Mood turns sour (unrest). When the union raises a written demand and management refuses, it becomes an industrial dispute.

5.2 Workers' participation in management (WPM)

WPM rests on industrial democracy: workers get a voice in decisions that affect them. Its aims are better trust and communication, higher morale and output, and fewer disputes.

FormLevelBasis
Works Committee (Sec. 3, ID Act)Plant; 100+ workmen; workers' members not fewer than the employer'sStatutory, advisory
Joint Management Council, 1958Establishment: welfare, safety, trainingGovernment scheme
Shop and Joint Councils, 1975Shop floor and unitGovernment scheme
Worker-directorBoard levelScheme or enabling provisions

5.3 Binding force of agreements

  • A bipartite agreement (Sec. 18(1)) binds only its parties.
  • A settlement in conciliation (Sec. 18(3)) binds all parties and all present and future workmen of the establishment. Both are covered by Sec. 2(p) and must be in writing and signed.
Remember this. Unrest is a mood; a dispute is a defined, formal claim. Works Committee: 100+ workmen, Sec. 3.
Common mistakes. Calling a Works Committee a bargaining body. It is only for consultation.

6. Collective bargaining (Unit II)

Collective bargaining is negotiation between an employer and the workers' union on pay, hours and other service terms, ending in a collective agreement.

6.1 Quick notes

  • Features: collective, two-sided, continuing, flexible, backed by power but voluntary, and written down at the end.
  • Forms: distributive (one side's gain is the other's loss), integrative (both gain), productivity (pay linked to higher output), composite (pay plus non-pay issues), concessionary (give up some benefits for job security).
  • Levels: plant, industry or region, and national; it becomes tripartite when the government joins.
  • Better than direct action: no loss of wages or output, keeps goodwill, gives a lasting agreement, needs less state action.
  • Limits: needs a strong, recognised union; weak in unorganised work; may raise costs.
  1. Prepare: collect data and fix demands.
  2. Negotiate: talk, give and take.
  3. Draft the agreement.
  4. Ratify with the members.
  5. Apply, review and renegotiate.
Shivalik Garments. The union asks for a 10% rise. Management offers 6% plus an output bonus. After talks, both sign a productivity agreement. That is productivity bargaining.
Remember this. Distributive = win-lose; integrative = win-win. Process: prepare, negotiate, draft, ratify, apply.
Common mistakes. Writing only the definition. Add forms, process and why it beats direct action.

Important terms

TermPlain meaningExample
Collective agreementWritten outcome of bargainingWage agreement
Productivity bargainingPay tied to output gainBonus for 8% more output
RatificationMembers' approval of the dealVote at a union meeting

7. Direct action, discipline, grievances, turnover and absenteeism (Unit III)

7.1 Direct action

Direct action means pressing demands by collective pressure instead of talks or adjudication. It is a last resort after peaceful methods fail.

MethodMeaningPosition
StrikeWorkers stop work togetherRecognised and regulated (Sec. 2(q), 22, 23)
Go-slowDeliberately slow work, still drawing pay (covert)Grave misconduct
Work-to-ruleFollow every rule to the letter to slow workUnclear; may be treated as a strike in effect
GheraoWorkers surround managers and stop them leavingNot recognised; may attract criminal law
PicketingStanding at the gate to persuade othersLawful if peaceful
BoycottRefusing to deal with the employer or productLawful if peaceful; unlawful if a conspiracy to injure trade

7.2 Discipline and misconduct

  • Discipline is orderly conduct by employees as per rules and reasonable orders. It can be positive (self-imposed, from understanding and good leadership) or negative (enforced by punishment).
  • Misconduct is an act or omission that breaks service terms, standing orders or accepted behaviour. Standing orders list it (for example disobedience, theft, damage, bribery, habitual absence, riotous conduct, illegal strike, go-slow).
  • Domestic enquiry: charge-sheet; reply by employee; enquiry with natural justice; findings; fitting punishment. If natural justice is broken, the Labour Court may look at the case afresh.

7.3 Grievance handling

A grievance is any real or felt dissatisfaction about one's job that an employee thinks unfair. Sources: working conditions, pay, supervision, rule violations, relations with co-workers.

The model procedure came from the Indian Labour Conference in 1958 [CHECK]. A good procedure prevents disputes.

7.4 Labour turnover and absenteeism

  • Labour turnover = (Employees separated ÷ Average employees) × 100. Causes: low pay, poor conditions, little growth (avoidable); retirement, death, illness (unavoidable). Effects: higher hiring and training cost, lost output and morale.
  • Absenteeism = (Man-days lost ÷ Man-days scheduled) × 100. Causes: ill health, dull work, poor supervision, weak welfare, debt. Controls: better conditions, fair leave, attendance incentives, counselling.
BasisTurnoverAbsenteeism
MeaningPermanent exit and replacementTemporary, unauthorised absence
EmployeeLeaves the rollsStays on the rolls
RemedyRetention policy, exit interviewsWelfare, incentives, counselling
Shivalik Garments. In a year, 30 workers leave a factory with an average strength of 400. Turnover = 30 ÷ 400 × 100 = 7.5%. In one month 600 man-days are lost out of 10,000 scheduled. Absenteeism = 6%.
Remember this. Go-slow is hidden, strike is open. Turnover = exit; absenteeism = absence.
Common mistakes. Treating go-slow as a lawful strike, and confusing turnover with absenteeism.

8. Strikes and lock-outs (Unit III)

8.1 Meaning

  • Strike (Sec. 2(q), ID Act): a stoppage of work by a body of persons acting together, or a joint refusal to continue work. Elements: stoppage or refusal, by a group, under a common understanding.
  • Lock-out (Sec. 2(l)): the employer closes the workplace, suspends work or refuses to continue employing workers. It is the employer's act, aimed at forcing workers to accept terms.

8.3 Restrictions and illegal strikes

  • Sec. 22 (public utility): no strike or lock-out without notice within six weeks before; within 14 days of notice; before the date stated in the notice; or during conciliation before an officer and seven days after.
  • Sec. 23 (all establishments): not during conciliation before a Board and seven days after; during proceedings before a Labour Court or Tribunal and two months after; during arbitration (when notified) and two months after; or while a settlement or award is in force on those matters.
  • Illegal (Sec. 24): begun or continued against Sec. 22 or 23, or continued against a government order under Sec. 10(3) or 10A(4A). A strike legal at the start does not turn illegal just by continuing.
  • Penalties: Sec. 26 for illegal strike or lock-out; Sec. 27 for instigation; Sec. 28 for financial aid.
  • Under the IR Code 2020 the notice rules reach all industrial establishments, not only public utilities [CHECK].

8.4 Legal vs justified

8.5 Protection of workmen

  • Sec. 33: no change in service terms or dismissal during proceedings without permission of the authority.
  • Sec. 33A: a workman may complain directly to the Labour Court if Sec. 33 is broken.
  • Protection from victimisation for lawful union activity (unfair labour practice, Fifth Schedule).
  • Lay-off, retrenchment and closure compensation soften the blow of disputes.
Shivalik Garments. Conciliation of the factory's wage dispute is under way before a Board. If workers walk out at that time, the strike is illegal under Sec. 23 even if their cause is fair.
Remember this. Strike = workers' stoppage; lock-out = owner's closure. Illegal is statute (Sec. 24); unjustified is courts' view.
Common mistakes. Mixing legality with justification, and forgetting the periods after proceedings end.

9. Trade unionism in India: growth, functions and problems (Unit IV)

A trade union (Sec. 2(h), Trade Unions Act 1926) is a combination, temporary or permanent, formed mainly to regulate relations between workmen and employers, among workmen, or among employers, and includes a federation of unions.

9.1 Origin and growth

  • Early phase: factory work grew under British rule, but organised unions were nearly absent; welfare efforts were individual.
  • 1918-1924: Madras Labour Union (1918), the first organised union; AITUC (1920), the first central body; India joined the ILO in 1919.
  • Legal phase: the Trade Unions Act 1926 allowed registration and gave immunity for acts in a trade dispute (Secs. 17 and 18).
  • Splits (to 1947): ideological differences led to splits; INTUC arose in 1947.
  • Features: late and slow growth; leadership from outside (lawyers, politicians); fragmentation.

9.2 After independence

UnionYearLink
INTUC1947Congress
AITUC1920Communist Party of India
HMS1948Socialist
BMS1955Nationalist
CITU1970CPI(M), split from AITUC
  • Growth of PSUs and white-collar unions; many unions in one firm; heavy use of adjudication.
  • After 1991: PSU privatisation, contract and informal work, IT and gig work, and plant-level bargaining weakened union strength. Unions responded with productivity bargaining, joint all-India strikes and attempts to organise informal and gig workers. Union density has fallen overall.

9.3 Objectives, functions and role in bargaining

  • Objectives: economic (pay, bonus), social (dignity, welfare), political (influence on law), protective (against unfair treatment).
  • Functions: protective or militant; fraternal or welfare; economic or bargaining; political.
  • In bargaining: represents workers, negotiates, helps carry out agreements, educates members, and steadies relations.

9.4 Problems

  • Multiple, rival unions with no single recognition rule at the central level (some states and the IR Code have provisions [CHECK]).
  • Political links and outside leadership.
  • Weak finances from low subscriptions.
  • Lack of trained leaders for modern bargaining.
  • Many tiny unions (minimum seven members to register; 2001 amendment added further conditions).
  • Little cover for unorganised and gig workers.
  • Employer resistance and weak action on unfair labour practices.
Shivalik Garments. The factory has two unions, one linked to a political party and one independent. Management meets each separately, so neither speaks for all workers. This is multi-unionism.
Remember this. Functions: protect, welfare, bargain, politics. Problems: many unions, politics, outside leaders, weak money.
Common mistakes. Giving the wrong year or link for a central union. Learn the table.

10. Employee empowerment, industrial democracy and industrial peace (Unit V)

10.1 Employee empowerment

Employee empowerment means giving employees more freedom, information, resources and decision power over their own work.

  • Elements: information sharing; control over work methods; a say in decisions; accountability matched with authority; training.
  • Aims: use workers' ideas, quicker decisions, higher motivation, better quality.
  • Tools: quality circles, suggestion schemes, self-managed teams, job enrichment.
BasisEmpowermentWPM
LevelIndividual or teamCollective bodies
LawMostly voluntaryPartly statutory
ScopeOwn job areaPlant or firm policy
TimingContinuousPeriodic meetings

10.2 Industrial democracy

  • Industrial democracy means bringing voice, representation and shared decisions into the workplace.
  • Forms: WPM; collective bargaining; co-determination (worker seats with real power on boards, as in Germany); employee ownership and profit-sharing; fair grievance and discipline safeguards.
  • Importance: less alienation; trust; checks arbitrary power; extends democracy to work. Article 43A of the Constitution directs the State to promote worker participation in management.

10.3 Industrial peace

Industrial peace means few disputes, strikes and lock-outs, with working ways to settle differences. Ways to achieve it: strong preventive machinery (Works Committees, grievance steps, standing orders); real collective bargaining; participation and empowerment; quick conciliation, arbitration and adjudication; tripartite talks.

Shivalik Garments. The factory starts a quality circle on the stitching line. Workers suggest a simpler layout that cuts rejects. The team feels trusted, and complaints fall.
Remember this. Empowerment is personal and daily; WPM is collective and scheduled; both serve industrial democracy.
Common mistakes. Using empowerment and WPM as the same thing.

11. The International Labour Organisation (Unit V)

The ILO was set up in 1919 after the Treaty of Versailles and became the first specialised agency of the United Nations in 1946. India has been a member since 1919. Its base idea is that lasting peace needs social justice.

11.1 Quick notes

  • Objectives: social justice; better pay and conditions; freedom of association and collective bargaining; social protection and safety; end forced and child labour and discrimination.
  • Unique feature: a tripartite structure with equal voice to governments, employers and workers.
  • Instruments: Conventions bind a state once it ratifies them; Recommendations are non-binding guidance.
  • Influence on India: Indian Labour Conference and Standing Labour Committee follow the tripartite idea; some laws on hours, safety and equal pay draw on ILO standards.

12. Labour policy, legislation and the changing business environment (Unit V)

12.1 Labour policy

  • Base: Directive Principles, Articles 39, 41, 42, 43 and 43A (living wage, humane conditions, participation).
  • Aims: industrial peace; protection of workers, especially the weaker ones; fair and quick dispute machinery; growth with welfare; social security for unorganised workers.
  • Evolution: from a protective, state-led approach after independence to flexibility and ease of doing business after 1991, ending in four Labour Codes: Wages 2019, Industrial Relations 2020, Social Security 2020, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions 2020. They came into force on 21 November 2025 and replace many older laws, including the ID Act and Trade Unions Act.

12.2 Working of labour laws

AchievementsGaps
Broad cover of wages, disputes, safety, social security, unionsWeak inspection and enforcement, especially in small units
Set dispute machinery; less raw direct actionMany overlapping laws; compliance burden
Freedom of association and bargaining recognisedLittle reach to informal and gig work
Delays in courts; C87 and C98 not ratified; weak unions limit real worker voice

12.3 Changing business environment

  • Drivers: LPG reforms, technology and automation, gig and platform work, start-ups and services, contract and fixed-term work.
  • Effects: weaker traditional forums (Works Committees, industry unions); new worker groups and online voice; enterprise-level and productivity-linked bargaining; HR-led, individual relations.
  • Policy response: the Code on Social Security 2020 names gig and platform workers for certain benefits. Its working is still developing [CHECK].

13. Important questions and model answers

These are my prediction, not past papers. Mark values follow your practice material (3, 6 and 10 marks); tell me if your paper differs. Tap a question to see the answer. Long answers follow one pattern: opening line, main points, example, conclusion.

13.1 Three-mark questions

What is industrial relations? (Chapter 1)

Relationships among employers, employees, their unions and the State arising from work in industry. In the systems view, managers, workers and government agencies make the rules of the workplace.

Distinguish between the unitary and pluralist approaches. (Chapter 2)

Unitary: one team, one authority, conflict abnormal, unions unnecessary. Pluralist: many groups with fair interests, conflict normal and manageable, unions legitimate.

Differentiate certified and model standing orders. (Chapter 4)

Certified orders are framed by the employer and certified for one establishment. Model orders are issued by the government and apply until certified orders start and where they are silent.

Distinguish industrial unrest from industrial dispute. (Chapter 5)

Unrest is a general mood of dissatisfaction, undefined in the Act. A dispute is a formal difference under Sec. 2(k) of the ID Act, linked to employment or its terms.

Define a strike and a lock-out. (Chapter 8)

Strike (Sec. 2(q)): a joint stoppage or refusal to work by workmen. Lock-out (Sec. 2(l)): the employer closes the workplace, suspends work or refuses to employ.

Write any three problems of Indian trade unions. (Chapter 9)

Multi-unionism and rivalry; political links and outside leadership; weak finances. Also: small unions and little cover for gig and unorganised workers.

13.2 Six-mark questions with model answers

Explain the unitary, pluralist and Marxist approaches to IR. (6 marks) (Chapter 2)

Opening: Approaches differ on whether conflict between employer and worker is normal.

  1. Unitary: one team and one authority; conflict is abnormal; unions are not needed.
  2. Pluralist: a group of interests; conflict is normal and manageable by bargaining and law; unions are legitimate.
  3. Marxist: capital-labour class conflict; unions are tools of struggle; peace needs a change in ownership.

Example: A strike at Shivalik Garments is "agitators" to the first, a bargaining issue to the second and class conflict to the third.

Conclusion: Indian dispute law mainly follows the pluralist view.

What are standing orders? Explain how they are certified. (6 marks) (Chapter 4)

Opening: Standing orders are written rules on service conditions under the Standing Orders Act, 1946.

  1. Employer files five copies of the draft with the Certifying Officer.
  2. Union or workmen get a copy to object.
  3. After a hearing, the officer certifies, with changes if needed.
  4. Appeal to the Appellate Authority within 30 days.
  5. They start 30 days after copies are sent and are displayed in the workers' language.

Example: The factory's termination clause becomes binding once certified.

Conclusion: Certification brings certainty and prevents disputes.

Describe the forms of workers' participation in management in India. (6 marks) (Chapter 5)

Opening: WPM gives workers a voice in decisions and rests on industrial democracy.

  1. Works Committee (Sec. 3, ID Act): plant level, advisory, statutory.
  2. Joint Management Council (1958): welfare, safety, training.
  3. Shop and Joint Councils (1975): shop and unit level.
  4. Worker-directors on Boards in some enterprises.

Example: A monthly Works Committee at the factory deals with canteen and safety.

Conclusion: Success depends on management's sincerity and union cooperation.

Explain the steps in a grievance procedure. (6 marks) (Chapter 7)

Opening: A grievance is a felt injustice about one's job; a clear procedure settles it early.

  1. Oral complaint to the supervisor.
  2. Written to the department head.
  3. Grievance Committee.
  4. Head of the plant.
  5. Voluntary arbitration by agreement.

Example: A worker with a wrong pay slip starts with the supervisor and moves up only if needed.

Conclusion: A good procedure prevents disputes from growing.

Explain the functions of trade unions and their role in collective bargaining. (6 marks) (Chapter 9)

Opening: A trade union is a combination formed mainly to regulate relations at work (Sec. 2(h)).

  1. Protective or militant: defend members.
  2. Fraternal: welfare, credit, education.
  3. Economic: bargain on pay and conditions.
  4. Political: influence laws and policy.
  5. In bargaining: represent, negotiate, help carry out agreements, educate and steady relations.

Example: The union at the factory negotiates a bonus and then helps settle queries on it.

Conclusion: A strong, responsible union makes bargaining work.

13.3 Ten-mark questions with model answers

Define collective bargaining. Explain its forms, process and why it is better than direct action. (10 marks) (Chapter 6)

Opening: Collective bargaining is negotiation between employer and union to settle pay and service terms, ending in an agreement.

  1. Features: collective, continuing, flexible, voluntary, written.
  2. Process: prepare, negotiate, draft, ratify, apply and review.
  3. Forms: distributive, integrative, productivity, composite, concessionary.
  4. Levels: plant, industry, national; tripartite if government joins.
  5. Better than direct action: no loss of wages or output, keeps goodwill, durable result, less state action.
  6. Limits: needs strong recognised unions; weak in informal work.

Example: The factory and its union sign a productivity agreement: a 6% rise plus an output bonus.

Conclusion: Bargaining solves disputes by dialogue and keeps the working relationship alive.

Distinguish between legal and illegal strikes, and justified and unjustified strikes. Explain the restrictions under Secs. 22 and 23. (10 marks) (Chapter 8)

Opening: A strike is a joint stoppage of work (Sec. 2(q)); its legality and its justification are judged separately.

  1. Legal: follows Secs. 22 and 23. Illegal (Sec. 24): breaks them or a prohibitory order; penalties in Secs. 26 to 28.
  2. Justified: reasonable cause and peaceful steps tried; a court-made test linked to wages for the strike period.
  3. Four combinations are possible, for example legal but unjustified.
  4. Sec. 22: public utilities need six weeks' notice, no strike within 14 days of notice, none before the stated date, none during conciliation and seven days after.
  5. Sec. 23: none during conciliation board, tribunal or arbitration proceedings and for set periods after, or while a settlement or award applies.
  6. The IR Code 2020 widens notice rules [CHECK].

Example: Workers at the factory strike during Labour Court proceedings: illegal under Sec. 23, even if the cause is genuine.

Conclusion: Legality is a statute test, justification a court test.

Discuss the objectives, structure and standard-setting role of the ILO. (10 marks) (Chapter 11)

Opening: The ILO (1919) is the UN's tripartite agency for labour standards, based in Geneva.

  1. Objectives: social justice; better conditions; freedom of association and bargaining; social protection; end forced and child labour and discrimination.
  2. Structure: International Labour Conference (2 government, 1 employer, 1 worker delegate per state), Governing Body, International Labour Office.
  3. Standards: Conventions bind after ratification; Recommendations guide.
  4. Key Conventions: C87, C98, C144, C154, C29, C105, C100, C111, C138, C182.
  5. India: ratified several core ones but not C87 and C98 [CHECK].
  6. Influence: tripartite bodies and some labour laws.

Example: If India ratifies a Convention, it must report on how it applies it.

Conclusion: The ILO shapes labour policy through tripartite standard setting.

16. Exam tips

  • Time: plan one mark per minute. Start with the answers you know best.
  • 3 marks: definition plus two or three points, or a small table.
  • 6 marks: opening line, three to five headed points, a one-line example, a conclusion.
  • 10 marks: add a table or figure, section numbers, and a balanced ending.
  • What examiners look for: correct section numbers, the structure asked for, tables for "distinguish" questions, and examples.
  • Common errors: mixing legal and justified strikes, calling Works Committees bargaining bodies, wrong years of union bodies, and ignoring the Labour Codes note.

17. Quiz: 20 questions

Tap a question to see the answer.

Q1. Which approach sees conflict as abnormal?
(a) Pluralist
(b) Marxist
(c) Unitary
(d) Systems

Answer: (c) Unitary. Unitary treats conflict as a fault of communication.

Q2. The three actors in Dunlop's model are:
(a) Managers, workers, government agencies
(b) Owners, banks, insurers
(c) Judges, police, lawyers
(d) Buyers, sellers, brokers

Answer: (a) Managers, workers, government agencies. Managers, workers and their groups, and government agencies.

Q3. The Standing Orders Act was passed in:
(a) 1926
(b) 1946
(c) 1947
(d) 1958

Answer: (b) 1946. 1946; 1926 is the Trade Unions Act and 1947 the ID Act.

Q4. Draft standing orders go to the:
(a) Labour Court
(b) Registrar of Unions
(c) Certifying Officer
(d) Conciliation Officer

Answer: (c) Certifying Officer. The Certifying Officer certifies them.

Q5. An appeal against certification lies within:
(a) 7 days
(b) 15 days
(c) 30 days
(d) 60 days

Answer: (c) 30 days. 30 days to the Appellate Authority.

Q6. A Works Committee under Sec. 3 applies to:
(a) 50+ workmen
(b) 100+ workmen
(c) 200+ workmen
(d) 500+ workmen

Answer: (b) 100+ workmen. Sec. 3 refers to 100 or more workmen.

Q7. Joint Management Councils began in:
(a) 1947
(b) 1958
(c) 1975
(d) 1991

Answer: (b) 1958. The JMC scheme dates from 1958.

Q8. A settlement under Sec. 18(3) binds:
(a) Only the union
(b) Only the employer
(c) All parties and all present and future workmen
(d) Only the government

Answer: (c) All parties and all present and future workmen. Conciliation settlements bind all concerned.

Q9. Bargaining where one side's gain is the other's loss is:
(a) Integrative
(b) Distributive
(c) Productivity
(d) Composite

Answer: (b) Distributive. That is distributive bargaining.

Q10. A go-slow is best described as:
(a) Overt and lawful
(b) Covert and treated as grave misconduct
(c) A lock-out
(d) A picket

Answer: (b) Covert and treated as grave misconduct. It hides behind normal attendance.

Q11. Sec. 2(q) of the ID Act defines:
(a) Lock-out
(b) Strike
(c) Dispute
(d) Settlement

Answer: (b) Strike. Sec. 2(q) is strike; 2(l) is lock-out.

Q12. A strike begun during conciliation before a Board is:
(a) Illegal under Sec. 23
(b) Legal under Sec. 22
(c) Always justified
(d) A lock-out

Answer: (a) Illegal under Sec. 23. Sec. 23 bars strikes during such proceedings.

Q13. Absenteeism rate is:
(a) Separations ÷ Average staff
(b) Man-days lost ÷ Man-days scheduled
(c) Profit ÷ Sales
(d) Leave ÷ Pay

Answer: (b) Man-days lost ÷ Man-days scheduled. It counts man-days lost, not exits.

Q14. A factory of 400 had 30 separations. Turnover is:
(a) 3.0%
(b) 7.5%
(c) 10%
(d) 13.3%

Answer: (b) 7.5%. 30 ÷ 400 × 100 = 7.5%.

Q15. The first organised trade union of India was formed in:
(a) 1881
(b) 1918
(c) 1947
(d) 1970

Answer: (b) 1918. Madras Labour Union, 1918.

Q16. CITU was formed in:
(a) 1948
(b) 1955
(c) 1970
(d) 1991

Answer: (c) 1970. CITU, 1970, after a split in AITUC.

Q17. Which is a function of trade unions?
(a) Auditing accounts
(b) Protective or militant
(c) Issuing patents
(d) Setting tax rates

Answer: (b) Protective or militant. Protective, fraternal, economic and political.

Q18. Article 43A refers to:
(a) Free legal aid
(b) Worker participation in management
(c) Right to property
(d) Primary education

Answer: (b) Worker participation in management. It directs the State to secure worker participation.

Q19. Each ILO member sends to the Conference:
(a) 1 delegate
(b) 2 delegates
(c) 4 delegates
(d) 10 delegates

Answer: (c) 4 delegates. 2 government, 1 employer, 1 worker.

Q20. The four Labour Codes came into force on:
(a) 1 April 2020
(b) 21 November 2025
(c) 15 August 2023
(d) 1 January 2024

Answer: (b) 21 November 2025. All four took effect on 21 November 2025.
